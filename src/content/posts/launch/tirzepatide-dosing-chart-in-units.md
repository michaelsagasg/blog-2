## Why units instead of milligrams

Reconstituted tirzepatide is drawn up with an insulin syringe marked in units, not millilitres or milligrams. A dosing chart in units exists to bridge that gap: it tells you how many units on the syringe correspond to a given mg dose, based on how much bacteriostatic water was used to reconstitute the vial.

### The maths behind the chart

The concentration after reconstitution depends entirely on how much liquid was added to the powder. A 10mg vial mixed with 2mL of bacteriostatic water gives 5mg per mL, or 0.5mg per 10 units on a standard 100-unit insulin syringe. Change the water volume and every number on the chart changes with it, which is why a chart is only correct for the specific reconstitution ratio it was built for.

## Reading the chart correctly

A properly labelled chart states the vial size, the water volume used, and the resulting concentration before listing dose-to-unit conversions. If a chart is missing any of those three numbers, the unit values on it can't be trusted, even if the final numbers look reasonable.

### A worked example

For a 10mg vial mixed with 2mL of water (5mg/mL), on the safe 1mg-to-4mg schedule: 1mg is 20 units, 2mg is 40 units, 3mg is 60 units, and the 4mg maintenance dose is 80 units. These numbers only hold for that exact 2mL mix; a 1mL mix would double the concentration and halve every unit figure.

## Before trusting any chart

Cross-check a chart against the vial's own label and the actual water volume used, not just the mg total. A chart copied from a different reconstitution ratio is the most common source of dosing errors reported in community discussions.
