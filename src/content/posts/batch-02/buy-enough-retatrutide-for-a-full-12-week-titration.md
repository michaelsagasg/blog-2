## Add up each stage separately, then decide whether to buy it all at once

A safe, general approach to titration is to start at 1mg in week one and increase by 1mg each week until reaching a 4mg maintenance dose in week four, then holding that dose for the rest of a 12-week schedule. Because the four ramp-up weeks use less product than the maintenance weeks that follow, working out the ramp-up and maintenance stages separately, then adding them together, gives a more accurate total than a flat "maintenance dose times 12 weeks" calculation would. ### The maths behind a typical 12-week schedule

Week 1 at 1mg, week 2 at 2mg, week 3 at 3mg and week 4 at 4mg totals 10mg for the ramp-up. Weeks 5-12 at the 4mg maintenance dose add a further 32mg. Added together, that's 42mg across the full 12 weeks, a little less than assuming the maintenance dose applied throughout, which would suggest 48mg.

## Buying it all upfront vs stage by stage

### The case for buying stage by stage

Buying only what the four-week ramp-up needs, then ordering separately for the maintenance stage, means never holding reconstituted product longer than necessary, and it leaves room to hold a step longer if side effects call for a slower pace without having overcommitted to a rigid schedule.

### The case for buying the full amount upfront

Ordering the full 42mg (or whatever the actual calculated total is) in one go can reduce shipping costs and avoids the risk of a mid-schedule reorder landing with a different batch. This only makes sense if vials can remain unreconstituted, and therefore more stable, until each stage is actually reached.

## Working with a supplier's actual vial sizes

### Rounding to available sizes

Suppliers don't always offer vial sizes that divide evenly into each stage's exact mg requirement. Round up to the nearest available size for each stage, accepting a small amount of leftover margin rather than trying to hit the number exactly.

### Checking concentration consistency

If planning to buy several vials across a 12-week schedule, check whether the concentration (mg per ml) stays consistent across sizes and reorders, since a change partway through changes the draw volume calculation for the remaining stages.

## Add a margin, but not too much

A 10-15% margin across the total calculated need is reasonable to account for draw loss during preparation. Padding much beyond that starts to create genuine waste risk, particularly for the earlier, low-dose stages where reconstituted product sits longer relative to how much is used each week.

## A reasonable way to structure the purchase

Buying in two batches, for example the four-week ramp-up upfront and the maintenance weeks closer to when they're needed, from a supplier like Enzo Meds that documents each batch clearly, balances the convenience of fewer orders against the risk of holding reconstituted product too long.

## Extending the same maths past 12 weeks

### Continuing at the maintenance dose

Since 4mg is meant to be held as an ongoing maintenance dose rather than stepped up further, extending past 12 weeks is simply a matter of multiplying 4mg by however many additional weeks are planned, no further dose increases involved.

### Why a fixed 12-week assumption can undersell what's needed

Treating 12 weeks as the entire schedule, when it's actually going to continue at the maintenance dose for longer, can lead to under-planning later purchases. Checking how many maintenance weeks are actually planned, not just the first 12, avoids this gap.

## Keeping the calculation flexible

If a step in the ramp-up ends up held longer than a week due to side effects, recalculate the total for that week using the actual time held before ordering the next vial. ## The bottom line

Work out total retatrutide needed for a 12-week titration schedule stage by stage, not by multiplying the maintenance dose across the whole period, then decide whether to buy it all upfront or in batches based on how comfortable you are holding reconstituted product for the full stretch.

Tracking mg purchased against mg actually used, updated after each order, helps catch a sizing error early rather than only noticing a mismatch once the schedule is well underway and harder to correct without an awkward top-up order.
