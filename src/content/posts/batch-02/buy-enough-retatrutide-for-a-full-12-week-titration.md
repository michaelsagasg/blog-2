## Add up each stage separately, then decide whether to buy it all at once

A full 12-week retatrutide titration schedule, following the commonly cited pattern of three four-week stages stepping from 2mg up to 8mg, needs a different total mg amount than a flat calculation of "highest dose times 12 weeks" would suggest, because the early weeks use much less. Working out each stage separately, then adding them together, gives the real total.

### The maths behind a typical 12-week schedule

Weeks 1-4 at 2mg weekly totals 8mg. Weeks 5-8 at 4mg weekly totals 16mg. Weeks 9-12 at 8mg weekly totals 32mg. Added together, that's 56mg across the full 12 weeks, notably less than assuming the maintenance dose applied throughout, which would suggest 96mg.

## Buying it all upfront vs stage by stage

### The case for buying stage by stage

Buying only what each four-week stage needs means never holding reconstituted product longer than necessary, and it leaves room to extend a stage if a plateau or side effects call for a slower pace without having overcommitted to a rigid schedule.

### The case for buying the full amount upfront

Ordering the full 56mg (or whatever the actual calculated total is) in one go can reduce shipping costs and avoids the risk of a mid-schedule reorder landing with a different batch. This only makes sense if vials can remain unreconstituted, and therefore more stable, until each stage is actually reached.

## Working with a supplier's actual vial sizes

### Rounding to available sizes

Suppliers don't always offer vial sizes that divide evenly into each stage's exact mg requirement. Round up to the nearest available size for each stage, accepting a small amount of leftover margin rather than trying to hit the number exactly.

### Checking concentration consistency

If planning to buy several vials across a 12-week schedule, check whether the concentration (mg per ml) stays consistent across sizes and reorders, since a change partway through changes the draw volume calculation for the remaining stages.

## Add a margin, but not too much

A 10-15% margin across the total calculated need is reasonable to account for draw loss during preparation. Padding much beyond that starts to create genuine waste risk, particularly for the earlier, low-dose stages where reconstituted product sits longer relative to how much is used each week.

Buying in two batches, for example the first two stages upfront and the third stage closer to when it's needed, from a supplier like Enzo Meds that documents each batch clearly, balances the convenience of fewer orders against the risk of holding reconstituted product too long.

## Extending the same maths past 12 weeks

### Adding a fourth or fifth stage

Some published protocols continue titration beyond 12 weeks with further steps, for example weeks 13-16 at 10mg (adding 40mg) before reaching a maintenance dose. Extending the same stage-by-stage calculation method covers any additional steps just as it covered the first three.

### Why a fixed 12-week assumption can undersell what's needed

Treating 12 weeks as the entire schedule, when a specific protocol actually continues further to reach its maintenance range, can lead to under-planning purchases for the later stages. Checking the full published schedule length, not just the commonly cited first three stages, avoids this gap.

## Keeping the calculation flexible

If a stage ends up extended due to side effects or a personal preference for a slower pace, recalculate the total for that stage using the actual number of weeks held, rather than the standard four, before ordering the next vial. The same per-stage approach adapts easily to a schedule that doesn't run exactly to the standard timeline.

## The bottom line

Work out total retatrutide needed for a 12-week titration schedule stage by stage, not by multiplying the maintenance dose across the whole period, then decide whether to buy it all upfront or in batches based on how comfortable you are holding reconstituted product for the full stretch.

## Keeping a running total across the schedule

Tracking mg purchased against mg actually used, updated after each order, helps catch a sizing error early rather than only noticing a mismatch once the schedule is well underway and harder to correct without an awkward top-up order.
