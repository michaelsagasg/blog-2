## Buy the smallest vial size that covers the first four weeks

For a first retatrutide purchase, the vial size that makes the most sense is the smallest option that reliably covers the starting dose held over the first stage, commonly around four weeks in published titration schedules. Buying a large vial as a first purchase, before knowing how the first stage goes, usually means holding more reconstituted product than gets used within its reliable window.

### Why the first purchase should be conservative

The starting dose in most published retatrutide protocols is low, commonly around 2mg, specifically because this is the amount used to introduce the substance gradually while assessing tolerability. A first-time buyer doesn't yet know how their first few weeks will go, so sizing the first purchase to match just that stage, rather than the whole schedule, is the more sensible approach.

## Working out the numbers

### Calculating total mg needed

Multiply the weekly starting dose by the number of weeks the first stage is typically held to get a rough total mg figure, then add a small margin (10-15%) for draw loss. Compare that total against the vial sizes a supplier actually offers.

### Concentration changes the answer

The same mg target can come from very different volumes depending on the vial's concentration. Always check mg per ml on the specific listing rather than assuming a "small" vial size necessarily matches the low starting dose you need.

## What to look for in a first-purchase listing

### A batch-specific COA

Since this is likely also the first time verifying a specific supplier, checking for a certificate of analysis tied to the exact batch being shipped matters even more than usual. A generic COA reused across every listing tells you nothing about the specific vial arriving at your door.

### Clear concentration labelling

A listing that states mg per ml plainly, rather than only listing total vial content, makes it far easier to confirm the size actually matches the starting dose stage before ordering.

## Avoiding common first-purchase mistakes

### Buying too large "to save on shipping"

Ordering a large vial upfront to avoid a second delivery fee often backfires, since reconstituted retatrutide has a limited window in which it's considered reliable, and a large vial used slowly during the low-dose stage may not be used up within that window.

### Buying too small and needing to reorder mid-stage

Undersizing is the opposite problem: a vial that runs out before the first stage is complete forces an unplanned reorder, potentially with a different batch, right in the middle of a schedule. Calculating the total mg needed before ordering avoids both extremes.

## A worked sizing example

### Doing the calculation

At a 2mg weekly starting dose held for four weeks, total mg needed is 8mg. Adding a 15% margin for draw loss brings that to roughly 9.2mg. If a supplier's smallest available size is 10mg, that's the size to buy; if the smallest available is significantly larger, such as 20mg, it's worth checking whether a smaller option exists elsewhere before committing to a vial that will be used slowly across the whole first stage.

### Checking the result makes sense

Once you've picked a vial size, divide its total mg by the weekly dose to confirm it covers at least four weeks with the intended margin. This quick sanity check catches sizing mistakes before they turn into a mid-stage reorder.

## What to do once the first vial is nearly finished

Start planning the second purchase, sized for the next titration stage's higher dose, once the first vial is about a week from running out, allowing enough time for delivery before the step-up date arrives.

The right retatrutide vial size to buy first is the smallest one that comfortably covers the published starting dose held over the first titration stage, calculated from the vial's actual concentration rather than assumed from its size label alone.
