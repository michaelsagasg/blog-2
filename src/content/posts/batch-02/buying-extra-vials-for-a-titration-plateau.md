## Plan for a possible plateau, but don't over-order upfront

A plateau, where a dose stage is held longer than originally planned because progress or tolerability hasn't changed, is a commonly reported part of titration for some people. It's reasonable to plan for this possibility, but buying a large stockpile of extra vials before it actually happens usually creates more waste risk than it solves.

### What a plateau actually means for buying

If a dose stage extends past its typical four weeks, the practical effect is that a vial sized for that stage gets used more slowly than planned. The buying question isn't really "extra vials" in the sense of stockpiling, it's whether the vial already purchased has enough left, or whether a small top-up order is needed.

## A better approach than stockpiling

### Buy in smaller increments generally

Rather than buying a large amount upfront specifically to cover a possible plateau, buying in smaller, more frequent amounts throughout a titration schedule naturally leaves room to extend a stage without having committed to unused product elsewhere.

### Keep a supplier relationship that supports quick reorders

A supplier with reliable UK delivery times and consistent batch documentation makes a small top-up order, if a plateau does happen, straightforward rather than stressful. This matters more than having pre-bought extra stock sitting in a fridge.

## When buying a bit extra does make sense

### If delivery times are unpredictable

If a supplier's delivery window is inconsistent, having a small buffer, enough for an extra week or two at the current dose, can be a reasonable hedge against a plateau colliding with a slow delivery. This is different from bulk stockpiling months ahead.

### If a batch is being discontinued

If a supplier flags that a specific batch or product size is being phased out, buying slightly more of the current, already-verified batch can make sense, since a plateau combined with a forced batch change adds an extra variable to track.

## What to avoid

### Buying months of stock speculatively

Reconstituted product has a limited reliable window, and even unreconstituted vials aren't meant to sit indefinitely. Buying far more than a realistic worst-case plateau would need risks product going unused past when it's still considered reliable.

### Treating a plateau as a reason to change dose without reference

A plateau in progress isn't itself a signal to increase a dose faster than the standard pattern. Buying more product doesn't change what the body is doing; it just means having enough on hand if the current stage needs to run longer than expected.

## A reasonable buying pattern

Ordering in amounts that comfortably cover the current stage plus a modest buffer, from a supplier like Enzo Meds that documents batches clearly and ships promptly within the UK, covers most plateau scenarios without the downsides of a large speculative stockpile.

## How to size a sensible buffer

### A modest extra amount, not a full extra stage

Rather than buying enough for an entire additional stage in advance, a buffer of roughly one to two extra weeks at the current dose is generally sufficient to cover a plateau without significant excess. This can be built into an order as a slightly larger vial size than the strict four-week calculation would suggest.

### Recalculating if a plateau is confirmed

If a plateau does happen and looks likely to continue for several more weeks, it's more sensible to place a proper top-up order sized for that extension at that point, rather than having tried to guess the exact extra amount needed from the outset.

## Keeping storage in mind through a plateau

If a plateau means a vial is reconstituted for longer than originally planned, check the vial is still within its supplier-stated reliable window before continuing to use it. If it's approaching the end of that window, a fresh vial at the same dose is the safer choice rather than stretching the existing one further.

## The bottom line

A small buffer beyond the calculated need for the current titration stage is a reasonable hedge against a plateau, but a large stockpile bought in anticipation usually isn't worth the risk of unused product sitting past its reliable window. A supplier who reorders quickly and consistently matters more than pre-bought stock.
