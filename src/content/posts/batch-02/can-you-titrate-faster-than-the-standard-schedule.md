## A faster pace is possible mechanically, but it's linked to more side effects, not better outcomes

There's nothing physically stopping someone from moving through titration steps faster than the standard one-week hold, but published data on GLP-1-based agonists consistently links faster dose increases to a higher rate of gastrointestinal side effects, without corresponding evidence of a meaningfully better outcome for reaching the pace sooner. The safe schedule's pace, 1mg to 4mg over four weekly steps, exists because of what the data showed about tolerability, not as an arbitrary default.

### What "faster" typically means

Faster titration usually means increasing the dose more than once a week, or skipping an intermediate step entirely and moving straight to a higher dose. Both approaches introduce dose increases more abruptly than the safe, general pace.

## What the data actually shows about pace

### Side effects tracked with the rate of increase

In trial data for GLP-1, GIP, and combined agonists, arms using faster dose escalation generally showed higher rates of nausea, vomiting, and discontinuation due to side effects compared with more gradual escalation arms reaching the same eventual dose.

### No clear evidence of better results from speed

Reaching a given dose faster hasn't been shown to produce better outcomes than reaching the same dose on the standard timeline, once a maintenance dose is established. The main documented difference is in the side effect profile during the titration period itself, not in the destination.

## Why some people titrate faster anyway

### Prior experience with a similar substance

Someone who has previously used a related GLP-1 medication and tolerated it well might feel more confident moving faster. This is a personal judgement call and isn't something published general information can validate or recommend for any specific individual.

### Impatience with a four-week ramp-up

Even a four-week schedule to reach the 4mg maintenance dose can feel slow, and the temptation to speed it up is understandable, even though it works against the reasoning behind the standard pace. ## What a slower-than-standard approach looks like by comparison

Extending a hold beyond one week, the opposite adjustment, is a commonly reported variation among people who find side effects haven't settled by the standard mark. This is a much better-documented and lower-risk adjustment than compressing the schedule.

## What this means for sourcing

A faster pace changes purchasing needs meaningfully: vials would need to be sized and ordered for a shorter runway per step, and a supplier's delivery speed becomes more critical if steps are only a few days apart instead of a full week. This is a practical planning consideration separate from whether a faster pace is itself well supported by the underlying data.

## What faster titration means for vial sizing

### Shorter steps need smaller, more frequent orders

If a faster pace is being considered, each step's vial should be sized for the shorter hold period, not the standard one week, since a vial sized for a full week used over a few days would leave a meaningful amount unused when the dose changes again.

### Delivery speed becomes more critical

A compressed schedule leaves less buffer time between ordering and needing the next step's vial. This makes a supplier's typical UK delivery time a more important factor than it would be on a standard-paced schedule with more breathing room between steps.

## Why slowing down is the better-documented alternative

If the goal behind considering a faster pace is discomfort with how long the standard four-week ramp-up takes, extending a hold when needed (the opposite adjustment) has considerably more support than compressing the schedule does, and doesn't carry the same documented side effect trade-off.

## The bottom line

Titrating faster than the standard one-week-per-step schedule is possible, but published data on this drug class associates faster increases with a higher rate of gastrointestinal side effects and doesn't show a documented benefit to reaching the 4mg maintenance dose sooner.
