## Side effects are tied to how fast a dose rises, not just the dose itself

Titration schedules for retatrutide and tirzepatide start low and increase gradually because gastrointestinal side effects, nausea, reduced appetite, and occasional stomach upset, are strongly linked to how quickly the dose goes up, not simply to the final dose reached. A slow ramp gives the digestive system time to adjust at each level before the next increase arrives.

### What's actually happening in the body

GLP-1 receptor agonists, and the dual or triple agonists like tirzepatide and retatrutide, slow gastric emptying as part of how they work. A sudden large dose slows digestion sharply and abruptly, which is what tends to produce the strongest nausea and discomfort. A smaller dose held for several weeks lets that slowing happen more gradually, and tolerability tends to improve with continued exposure at a given level.

## Why trials use this pattern

### Trial data shaped the general approach

Gradual, weekly dose increases are the safe general pattern recommended for retatrutide and tirzepatide, reflecting the same trial-informed logic of balancing an effective dose against keeping side effects manageable enough to continue.

### It's not an arbitrary caution

The gradual pace isn't just a conservative default added for legal reasons, it reflects what actually produced better tolerability in trial populations. Faster ramp-up arms in some studies showed higher rates of side effects severe enough to cause discontinuation.

## What "go slow" means in practice

### One-week holds, rising by 1mg

Holding each dose level for a full week before increasing by 1mg gives enough time to judge whether side effects are settling at that level, rather than increasing before the body has had a chance to adjust.

## How this compares across similar medications

### A pattern seen across the wider GLP-1 class

The start-low, go-slow approach isn't unique to retatrutide or tirzepatide. Other GLP-1 receptor agonists licensed for various uses follow a similar gradual titration logic, because the underlying mechanism, slowed gastric emptying, and the side effects that come with it, are shared across the drug class rather than specific to any one molecule.

### Why this consistency matters

Seeing the same gradual titration pattern applied consistently across a class of medications, in both licensed prescribing information and self-sourced reference material, reinforces that this is a mechanism-driven design choice rather than an arbitrary convention specific to one product or supplier.
